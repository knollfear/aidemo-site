You Had One Job

Getting more from AI by doing less with AI.

scroll ↓

This started as a chore, not an architecture.

I wanted a website
for my kids.

That’s the whole origin story. And like any proper engineering effort, I started with requirements — what I wanted, and what I wasn’t willing to sign up for. The second list is the one that did the design work.

What I wanted

  • ✓Somewhere my kids can publish their own stuff
  • ✓Kid writes a sentence → it’s on the site. No ceremony, no ticket, no waiting on Dad
  • ✓Me as content police — that job I’ll happily take
  • ✓An AI angle — and I’ll be honest about why: my future employability apparently depends on how well I can convince an AI that I understand AI

What I didn’t

  • ✗Me as Head of IT. Or IT shadow.
  • ✗A monthly bill with a growth curve
  • ✗A CMS to patch, a database to back up, a plugin that breaks on January 19, 2038
  • ✗An AI that gets creative with the budget. Or the facts.

Cheap, boring, kid-proof — with exactly enough AI in it to be a period piece. The folder part will still work in 2038. Ask me about the AI part then.

The less you do, the more you do.

Most of an “AI” pipeline
isn’t AI.

This is a system that turns an email — “dear website, I got a new bike, here’s a photo” — into a live website update, in about two minutes.

It has exactly one seam of AI. Everything else is plain, boring, trustworthy code. That’s not a compromise. That’s the design.

This is the entire product.

I got a new bike

August 19, 2026

I got a new bike for my birthday! It’s blue and it has 21 gears. I rode it to the park with Dad.

One human click in the middle, to approve it. The model’s entire job was turning the left box into the right box’s words.

First, the boring part. It’s load-bearing.

The whole website
is a folder.

No database. No app. No server humming somewhere. This site is a folder of text files — and every idea in this talk is just a rule about who is allowed to change that folder, and when.

📁 Git

An undo button with a memory. Every change to the folder is saved as a labeled snapshot — who, what, when — forever. Track-changes, but for a folder.

🐙 GitHub

That folder, online, where changes get proposed rather than just made. A “pull request” is a suggested edit, sitting in a queue. “Here’s my change — okay?”

🌐 GitHub Pages

Free hosting. GitHub takes the folder and hands it to anyone who visits. Nothing to run, nothing to patch, nothing to pay. This page is that folder.

⚙️ GitHub Actions

A robot that watches the folder. An approved change lands, it rebuilds the site and publishes — about ninety seconds, hands off. Same checklist every time, or it fails loudly.

Why spend two minutes on that? Because it’s the entire safety story.

A dumb folder beats
a clever robot.

All four of those pieces are boring on purpose. Boring is exactly what makes it safe to let an AI near a kid’s website at all.

📁

A page that’s a file

  • Somebody read it before it went up
  • Every version is on the record — yesterday’s is one click away
  • Nothing runs when a visitor arrives, so nothing breaks at 2 a.m.
  • Costs $0, and outlives my interest in it
🤖

A page the AI serves

  • Written fresh for each visitor — nobody read it first
  • No version to roll back to; it just said something different
  • Down when the API is down
  • A bill, and a thing to babysit

So the AI never touches the live site. It proposes a change to a folder, a human says yes, and a robot ships it. That’s the whole trick.

The problem isn’t the cloud.
And it never was.

I love the cloud. This site is on it, for free. What I don’t need is nginx, in a bespoke Docker container, on a medium EC2 instance, in a VPC I hand-designed, in front of an Aurora database. That isn’t the cloud’s promise — that’s a datacenter I’m renting, with 487 new ways to be wrong.

True story — the one that made it a “medium”

That “medium” is not a typo. We ran a small instance until the day it couldn’t build the site anymore — ran out of memory generating static files. So I resized it. Paid more, every month, forever, so that a server I maintain could do a job that finishes in twenty seconds on somebody else’s free build runner — and produce files that never needed a server in the first place. Nobody in that meeting said the words “why do we have a server?” We said “resize it.” That’s knob thinking.

So what did I want instead?

I bring files.
Somebody else brings
every other decision.

GitHub Pages is the promise. I don’t configure it, patch it, or get paged by it. Somebody else made every architectural call it takes to serve a website — and lived with it at a scale I will never see.

Give me five rock-solid architectures that cover 99% of the web, and let me point at one.

And to be clear about who builds those five: DevOps engineers. I’m not asking for fewer of them. I’m asking them to solve it once, for everyone — instead of once per company. Or once per team. Or, and we both know this is the real number, once per project. Give a good engineer a knob and they will find a defensible reason to turn it — that isn’t a character flaw, that’s the job description. The fix was never better judgment. It was fewer knobs. Also, and I mean this sincerely: they could be outside.

Fewer knobs, fewer ways to be catastrophically wrong — the same bet I’m making with the AI.

I don’t wear a watch anymore. My phone tells the time.

The everything-device trap.

The LLM is the smartphone of your stack. It can do almost anything — a little. Which is exactly why you’re tempted to let it absorb every job.

⌛

A watch

Does one thing. Glanceable, cheap, never asks you to log in, never hallucinates the time.

  • parse an address
  • validate a slug
  • move a file, open a PR
📱

A phone

Also tells the time — while being a slot machine, a camera, and forty notifications.

  • understand “I got a new bike”
  • …and that’s it.

Don’t make a load-bearing clock out of a slot machine. Use a watch for the watch jobs.

Do less. Not nothing. You gotta do something.

So what is something?

Read a human sentence,
written by a 7-year-old,
and work out what it means.

I have been paid to write code in three different decades, and I would not know how to begin. The model does it on the first try, for a fraction of a cent.

That isn’t a risk I’m managing — it’s the reason this pipeline exists at all.

When life gives you lemons, say ‘forget the lemons’ and bail.

Bail to a human.

The four scariest words in the English language:
“Trust the model’s judgment.”

So we do not. The safety model is a path filter in CI — the agent can only touch content/ — and a pull request a human sees first. We chose it not because it is hard, but because it is easy.

If you seek peace of mind,
if you seek prosperity from lowered cloud spend,
if you seek liberation from AWS: Use this gate.

You can’t fight the wave. You’ve got to ride it.

Ride the model’s grain.

The agent writes markdown, never HTML. Its output runs through Pydantic before it touches a file — valid slug, real date, a section that exists.

Bodhi paddles out into a fifty-year storm, and a cop on the beach says “we’ll wait for him to come back in.” There is no coming back in. It was never one of the options.

Neither is HTML. I am not trusting the model to choose well — I removed the other choices. Same trade I made with GitHub Pages, pointed at the model instead of at me: fewer options, fewer ways to be wrong.

A confession from somewhere in the build.

I thought it outsmarted me.

I was about to commit every scrap in this folder — a megabyte and change of unused source images — into a public repo, forever, because sorting them felt like work. The agent committed the six files the page actually serves, and left the rest alone.

For about a second, that genuinely felt like intelligence.

You keep using that word.
I do not think it means what you think it means.

It was not intelligence. It was a rule — do not put unreferenced binaries in permanent history — applied at midnight exactly the way it would be applied at noon, by something that does not get tired and does not get lazy. That is not a new kind of mind. That is a computer. The one job is still the one job. But the part that saved me was the boring part.

And if you think these jokes are bad, you should see the ones I threw away. Doing less applies to material, too.

This is what it looks like.

Do less.

Find the seam where AI is genuinely the right tool. Give it that one job. Let honest code carry the rest.

The folder outlives the model.
